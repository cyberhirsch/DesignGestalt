Gestalt playground

Seven principles you can push until they break. Move each slider slowly and watch for the moment the grouping flips — that tipping point is the whole lesson, and it is much easier to feel than to be told. One of the seven hardly breaks at all.

Law of Proximity

Distance decides what belongs together

Identical dots, one variable: the gap between the columns. Close it and the field reads as rows; open it past the spacing of the rows and it snaps into columns. Nothing about the dots themselves changed.

Law of Similarity

Likeness beats position

Spacing stays perfectly even here. Raise the difference and the three middle columns peel away as a group, although they sit no closer to each other than to their neighbours. Shape changes at equal area; then switch to tone and size: which difference splits the grid first?

Law of Closure

The eye finishes the shape

Take the outline away and the eye keeps drawing the circle in. With one big gap it turns into a C quite soon. Spread the same loss over five uneven gaps and you still see a circle when most of it is missing. Find where each version stops being one.

Law of Continuity

A smooth path wins over a broken one

Identical dots, laid along two smooth paths. You read two lines passing through each other — never four arms meeting in the middle. Shake the dots loose and the lines hold for longer than you would expect, then dissolve into a scatter.

Law of Common Fate

Moving together beats standing near

The dots are identical and evenly spaced, so neither similarity nor proximity can group them. Raise the amplitude and six scattered dots start moving in step — a group appears out of nothing but shared motion, and disappears the moment they stop.

Law of Prägnanz

The simplest reading available wins

One fill, so all you see is the outline of the union: a shape nobody drew. You still read a circle and a square, because two simple shapes are a simpler story than one complicated one. Push them all the way together: the outline gets stranger, the reading barely moves. This is the law that hardly breaks.

Figure & Ground

Which half is the subject?

Rubin's vase: two faces, or a vase. Shift the balance and one reading takes over; the filled region becomes the subject. In the middle both compete — and that instability is exactly what a poster must never leave to chance.
